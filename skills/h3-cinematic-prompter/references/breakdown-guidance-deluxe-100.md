# Story Breakdown — Deluxe compact: Short Story → Acts → Scenes → Clips

From five books (Cowgill, Phillips, Johnson, Gurskis, Giglio), for a pipeline where a
clip = one continuous generated shot (4–8 s), a scene = clips in one place/time, an act
= a movement. A **story beat** = one unit of change ≈ one scene; a **shot beat** = one
attack/counter/pause/reaction inside a scene = one clip.

## Foundations

- Two engines: **conflict** (someone wants something and is having trouble getting it)
  and **connection** (stories also turn on moments of connection/disconnection —
  comedy ends in connection, tragedy in disconnection: test every ending's valence).
- Phrase the **dramatic question** as a question; every scene serves it, the climax
  answers it.
- **Want** must be specific to this plot (never "happiness"); the **need** is what the
  character couldn't tell you if asked; pursuing the want exposes the need, and endings
  often satisfy the need while the want is lost.
- The atoms of story are **discoveries and decisions** (discovery → deliberation →
  decision → doing); a dramatic choice must be able to alter a life or the world.
- **Emotion is the story** — vary it scene to scene; it flows across cuts.
- **Show, don't tell**: run "How Do We Know?" on every fact; mute the film and the plot
  should still track. Character is action; deliberation gets a clip.
- **Voltage rule**: the shorter the film, the less trauma it carries before melodrama.
  One main event per short; narrative cram kills — smaller is better.
- Arc scales with runtime: no pole-to-pole transformation under ~10 min — moderate
  change seeded before scene one, or an unchanged protagonist who changes others.
- Antagonist: hero of their own story, worthy opponent within this conflict; unity of
  opposites (mutually exclusive objectives). Confidant = the naturalistic outlet for
  interiority (prefer over VO). Cast: 2–3 min film ≈ two characters.

## Size, then map beats (two passes)

- Sizes (Gurskis): 2–4 min = 1–2 scenes, one crisis; 7–12 min ≈ 5–8 scenes; 20–25 min
  ≈ 12–15; 30+ ≈ 20–30. No B-plot in short-shorts or conventional shorts (≤12 min).
  Compress story time toward hours; add
  a time lock or condition lock. Pipeline: one story beat = one scene of 2–6 clips
  (≥3 shot beats); board under ~100 clips — cut named beats, never thin everything.
- **Pass 1 (step outline)**: number every story beat (~10–30) with its source passage,
  scanning for conflict AND connection beats; tag each +/− (tags must alternate); mark
  inciting incident, midpoint, crisis, climax, resolution, plants/payoffs, locks; test
  the chain reads "A, THEREFORE B, BUT C" — never "and then"; check each major event
  has a preparation beat before and reaction beat after; flag unfilmable passages.
- **Pass 2 (scene outline)**: per scene list every minor beat — that list becomes the
  clips.
- **Verify after drafting**: every story beat → ≥1 clip or consciously cut with a
  reason; bridge scenes (pure transitions) go first.

## Acts

- **Setup**: setting, tone, protagonist hint, and the inciting incident *ending* the
  setup (≈ quarter mark at latest, or pre-story). Three scenes max to open; under 15
  min, conflict by scene two. Plant now what the ending pays off.
- **Middle**: cause and effect; state the plan in one plain line; vary opposition
  (person, physical, inner, mystic/fate/chance/moral); alternate wins and losses;
  midpoint reversal or pseudo-solution; the middle is the promise of the premise and
  ends at the worst moment. Pick the knowledge dial per scene: mystery (audience knows
  less), suspense (same), dramatic irony (more — the short's cheapest tension).
- **Ending**: climax external — protagonist present, personally staked, a visible
  event, never internal. Shorts end on twist (true, recontextualizing — not cheap),
  irony, or revelation; open endings fine if chosen; no circular endings, no
  resolution-by-death. Resolution = one scene. Audiences forgive almost anything at
  the beginning and almost nothing at the end.

## Scenes

- Any location change or time jump = new scene. Every scene turns; one point,
  statable in one sentence; nothing changed → cut.
- Each scene has its own scene-protagonist, scene-objective, and opposition. The
  reliable engine is the **boxing match**: one wants what the other won't give — tie
  the want to a physical object.
- Enter late (action underway), leave early; no greetings or drive-ups; prefer
  face-to-face in one location over phone scenes unless the premise demands one. End
  on a hook answered in a later scene. Build least → most important; a scene may
  complete by implication in the next.
- Two pages preferred, three max; vary mood scene to scene.

## Clips

- One shot beat = one clip, one camera idea; ≥3 per scene; each new paragraph of
  action ≈ one shot. Never a whole scene in one clip; "fewer, denser" ≠ dropping beats.
- **Meaning lives between clips** (the Kuleshov/Eisenstein principle): order
  deliberately; a reaction shot after a reveal writes the emotion wordlessly. A held
  silent reaction is often the strongest beat.
- Vary shot size; no identical adjacent framings of the same subject (reverse angles
  fine); give the frame three concrete setting details; business is characterization;
  the turn must be visible in a clip.
- Prompts fully self-contained; never "same as before"; repeat
  prop/wardrobe/lighting/location anchors. Write the **specific gesture, not the named
  emotion** — no actor exists to interpret. No dialogue in video prompts unless the
  model renders speech. Image prompt = one still; `first-frame` vs `reference` chosen.
- Montage = 2–4 single-image clips compressing time; never montage what deserves a
  dramatized scene. End scenes and acts on their strongest image.

## Dialogue

- Gate: a line advances plot, reveals character, carries needed info, sets tone, or
  serves theme — or is cut. Write dialogue last, after the visual story works.
- One idea per speech; 1–2 lines; strongest word LAST (second-most first); break long
  speeches into exchanges; monologues only as motivated self-revelation.
- Text simple, subtext complex; never write what an actor could play. Exception: the
  **want** may be stated plainly once (the plan/"I want" line); feelings and theme,
  never. Exposition is earned through friction, never briefed.
- Distinct voice per character; techniques: signature phrase, indirectness, saying the
  opposite, echo line returning changed, Greek Chorus friend voicing doubts.
- No names between intimates; nod/shake replace yes/no; the last line is what the
  audience carries out. Format `NAME: line`, exact known character names.

## Voiceover

Job test + ladder: **dramatize > confidant scene > VO > direct address.** VO only for
compression, irony against the image, a told frame, the epistolary bridge (letters in
VO interlocking characters who never meet), poetic overlay, or binding vignettes.
Never to restate the image, deliver "and then I realized," or patch structure.
Convention (planning-only): `NARRATOR:` / `NARRATOR (CONT'D):`, never in `characters`;
span text split across clips' dialogue; duration ≈ words ÷ 2.5, ~10–20 words/clip
(≈4–8 s).

## Prose conversion + failure modes

- Interiority → reaction shot/business → confidant → VO (in that order). Summary →
  montage or cut the jump. Reported speech → the live scene. Stated feeling → the
  specific behavior. Backstory → one planted object; flashback only for revelation.
  What a character fails to do is filmable; the biggest beat can live off-screen in
  its reaction.
- Watch for: late inciting incident; too few major beats; unarticulated conflict;
  mismatched opponents; hasty resolutions; voltage overload; narrative cram; episodic
  drift; circular endings; silent compression (antidote: the two-pass map, verified);
  internal climax; cross-referencing clips; caps ≤8/≤12/≤12 (split acts, state
  merges); invented entities; revision drift.

## Shapes to steal

*Life Lessons* (locked pair, unchanged protagonist); *The Lunch Date* (near-wordless
irony, recontextualizing twist); *Owl Creek* (jeopardy in images, twist-as-climax);
*PEEL* (prop-driven standoff); *Ray's* (vignettes on ironic VO); *The String Bean*
(wordless, 42 scenes ≈ 24 s — clip-scale precedent); *Kosher* (5 pages of single-point
scenes); *Lena's Spaghetti* (epistolary VO); *Miracle Fish* (midpoint dramatic irony,
climax of connection); *Black Hole* (3-minute premise-promise-snap).
